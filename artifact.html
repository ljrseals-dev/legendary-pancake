Tube Singer

A singing voice made the way Bell Labs did it in 1961: not by filtering a buzz, but by simulating sound travelling down a tube shaped like a throat. Forty-four short tube sections, each reflecting a little of the wave back where the width changes. The vowels are tube shapes, and the song is Daisy Bell.

Voice

Tract length moves all the vowel resonances together (shorter is higher, like a smaller singer) without changing the pitch. Singer's formant shaping narrows the tube just above the larynx and widens the throat behind it, which pushes energy toward 3 kHz; it also lowers the first resonance a little, so vowels get darker.

Hold one vowel

Each key holds that vowel for 1.5 seconds on the middle note of the song at the current settings. The tube shapes were fitted by a numerical search to textbook-style average male formants, not measured from anyone's throat.

Method and honest notes

Each of the 44 sections is a short uniform tube; at each junction a fraction k = (A1 - A2) / (A1 + A2) of the wave is reflected. The glottis end reflects most of the wave back and the lip end reflects most of it with a little low-pass, so the lips pass high frequencies better, which is what a real mouth does. The source is a Rosenberg glottal pulse with vibrato, a little jitter and breath. Vowel shapes glide into each other over about 70 ms, and a syllable with two vowels (like "I") glides from the first to the second near its end.

What this is not. No consonants are modelled, so "Daisy" is sung as vowels only (a vowel with a little dip at each new syllable). The notes come from a public BASIC transcription of the tune (G major, 3/4); the syllable assignment is mine and the last lines are approximate. This is not a reconstruction of the Bell Labs program or its tract data. Nobody has listened to the result.
